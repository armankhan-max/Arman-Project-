# [Chapter 9] Clothing Care and Repair

LEAD: Good care doubles the life of clothes and keeps them looking new. Most of it takes minutes, and none of it needs special skill.

## Hanging and storing

| W=1.5,2.5,2 | Garment | How to store | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jackets and suits | Wide, shaped wooden hanger that fills the shoulders | Thin wire hangers (they leave bumps in the shoulders) |
| Trousers | Clamp hangers by the hem, or folded over a padded bar | Leaving belts in the loops |
| Shirts | Wooden or plastic hanger, top button fastened | Overcrowding the rail |
| Knitwear | Folded, on a shelf or in a drawer | Hanging: it stretches the shoulders |
| Coats | Broad wooden hanger, buttoned | Hanging heavy coats on hooks for months |
| Shoes | Cedar shoe trees, side by side, out of direct sun | Piled in a heap at the bottom of the wardrobe |

### Seasonal storage

[ ] Clean everything before storing it. Moths are attracted to food, sweat and skin oils, not clean wool.
[ ] Store wool in breathable cotton garment bags or boxes, not sealed plastic.
[ ] Add cedar blocks or lavender sachets to deter moths. Refresh cedar by lightly sanding it once a year.
[ ] Check stored wool every couple of months for small holes or tiny larvae.
[ ] Keep the storage cool, dry and dark.

!WARN Small irregular holes in wool, especially in folds or under the collar, are a sign of moths. Move the garment away from others, have it cleaned, and check everything nearby.

## Resting your clothes

- **Jackets and suits:** rest for at least a day between wears so the wool recovers its shape.
- **Shoes:** rest a day between wears so the leather dries out. Two pairs worn alternately last more than twice as long as one pair worn daily.
- **Empty the pockets** every evening. Hang the jacket, open, to air before putting it in the wardrobe.

## Brushing

A clothes brush is the most under-used tool in a man's wardrobe.

1. Hang the jacket or coat up.
2. Brush downwards with short, firm strokes, starting at the shoulders.
3. Pay attention to the collar, shoulders and sleeves, where dust and hair collect.
4. Do this after every few wears. It removes dirt before it settles into the cloth and reduces the need for dry-cleaning.

## Washing wool

Many wool sweaters can be hand-washed. Check the care label first.

1. Use a basin of **cool or lukewarm** water. Hot water shrinks wool.
2. Add a small amount of gentle wool wash or mild soap.
3. Turn the sweater inside out and press it gently into the water. Do not rub or scrub.
4. Soak for about ten minutes.
5. Rinse in cool water of the same temperature.
6. **Never wring.** Press the water out gently against the side of the basin.
7. Roll the sweater in a dry towel and press to absorb more water.
8. **Dry flat** on a fresh towel, away from radiators and direct sun. Gently reshape the shoulders, cuffs and hem.

!WARN Never hang a wet sweater. The weight of the water pulls it out of shape, and the shoulders and length will stretch permanently.

## Dealing with pilling

Pilling is the small fuzzy balls that form where fibres rub together: under the arms, on the elbows, at the sides.

1. Lay the sweater flat on a table.
2. Hold the knit taut with one hand.
3. Use a **sweater comb** or a **fabric shaver** in light, short strokes across the surface.
4. Work slowly. Do not press hard or stay in one place.
5. Brush the loose fuzz away.

| W=2,4 | Pilling level | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| A few small pills | Comb or shave as above; good as new |
| Pills all over, cloth still firm | De-pill; consider wearing it at home |
| Thin, see-through patches or holes | Retire it, or keep it for gardening |
| Cuffs and hem stretched and floppy | Retire it. Ribbing rarely recovers |

!TIP To reduce pilling: wash knitwear inside out, wear a cotton shirt rather than a rough one under it, and rotate sweaters so the same one is not rubbed every day.

## Ironing and pressing

### Shirts, in order

1. Iron while slightly damp. It is faster and gives a crisper finish.
2. **Collar** first: underside, then top side, working from the points inward.
3. **Cuffs**: inside, then outside.
4. **Sleeves**: lay flat, smooth, iron along the length.
5. **Back yoke** (the shoulder panel).
6. **Fronts and back**: work around the shirt.
7. Hang immediately and fasten the top button.

### Trousers

1. Iron pockets and waistband first, inside out.
2. Lay one leg flat, matching the inside and outside seams so the crease falls at the front centre.
3. Use a **pressing cloth** (a clean cotton tea towel) between the iron and wool to avoid shine.
4. Repeat for the other leg.

!WARN Never iron wool directly, especially dark wool. It leaves a shine that is very hard to remove. Always use a pressing cloth, and use steam rather than heavy pressure.

### Using a steamer

A simple handheld steamer removes creases from jackets, trousers and shirts without an ironing board. Hang the garment, hold the steamer a little away from the cloth and move it downwards.

## Polishing shoes

1. Remove the laces.
2. Brush off dust and dirt with a horsehair brush or damp cloth.
3. Let the shoe dry if you used water.
4. Apply a small amount of cream or wax polish in the matching colour with a soft cloth, using **small circles**.
5. Leave for about ten minutes.
6. Buff with a clean brush or soft cloth until it shines.
7. For extra shine on the toe, add a tiny amount of wax with a drop of water and buff again in small circles.
8. Re-lace and put the shoe trees in.

!TIP Once a month is enough for most shoes. Do it on a Sunday evening with the radio on: it becomes a pleasant ritual, and every pair you own will look well cared for.

## Replacing a button

You need: a needle, thread in the matching colour, and the button.

1. Thread the needle and double the thread so you have two strands. Knot the end.
2. Mark where the button goes: look for the old thread holes.
3. Push the needle up from the back through the cloth and through one button hole.
4. Place a pin or matchstick across the top of the button. This creates space so the button is not sewn too tight.
5. Sew through the holes, up and down, five or six times. On a four-hole button, follow the pattern of the other buttons (two parallel lines or a cross).
6. Remove the pin. Lift the button and wind the thread round the stitches underneath it four or five times to make a short *shank*.
7. Push the needle to the back, make several small stitches, and knot it off.

!NOTE Most good shirts and jackets come with spare buttons sewn inside or in a small bag. Keep them all in one jar so you can always find one.

## Small repairs you can do or ask for

| W=1.8,2.4,1.8 | Problem | Fix | Who |
|---|---|---|---|
| Loose button | Re-sew as above | You |
| Hem coming down | Hemming tape (iron-on) as a quick fix, or re-stitch | You or tailor |
| Small seam opening | A few stitches inside | You or tailor |
| Torn pocket lining | New pocket bag | Tailor |
| Moth hole in a sweater | Darning or a small patch on the elbow | Tailor or knitter |
| Worn elbows on a tweed jacket | Leather or suede elbow patches | Tailor |
| Frayed shirt collar | Turn the collar, or replace with spare cloth from the tail | Tailor |
| Broken zip | Replace the zip | Tailor |

## Removing the tacking threads

New jackets often come with pockets and the back vent loosely stitched shut, to keep their shape in the shop. They are meant to be removed.

1. Look for a small X of contrast thread across the vent at the back hem, and loose stitches along pocket openings.
2. Using small, sharp scissors, carefully snip the threads. Do not cut the cloth.
3. Pull out the loose threads with your fingers or tweezers.
4. Leave stitched pockets that are *sewn shut with matching thread inside the garment* until you have checked with a tailor; some decorative pockets are meant to stay closed.

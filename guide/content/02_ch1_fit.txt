# [Chapter 1] The Fit Checklist

LEAD: Five garments, one system. Learn these checks once and you can judge any jacket, pair of trousers, shirt, coat or sweater in under a minute, in a shop or in your own wardrobe.

## The three rules behind every check

Before the details, here is the logic. Every fit check in this chapter comes back to one of three rules.

1. **Structure points must match your body.** Shoulders, collar, waist and the length of sleeves and legs are the "anchor points" of a garment. If they line up with your body, everything in between hangs properly.
2. **Cloth should skim, not cling and not float.** You want the garment to follow your shape with a little room. Pulling means too tight. Folds and billowing mean too loose.
3. **Clean lines beat extra fabric.** Excess cloth at the ankle, wrist or seat adds visual weight and age. Removing it is the single cheapest way to look sharper.

!TIP Always check fit standing in front of a mirror *and* in a side view. Use your phone: set a 10-second timer, prop it on a shelf at waist height, and take one photo from the front, one from the side and one from the back. Photos show what the mirror hides.

## Part A: The jacket

The jacket matters most because it frames your face and shoulders, and because it is the garment people judge from furthest away. A jacket that fits makes a modest suit look expensive. A jacket that does not fit makes an expensive one look borrowed.

### Jacket check 1: Shoulders

The shoulder is the foundation. Everything else hangs from it, and it is the hardest part to change.

- The shoulder seam should end where your shoulder bone ends. Feel for the bone at the outer edge of your shoulder.
- The sleeve should fall straight down from the shoulder, without a dent, ripple or ridge.
- From the front, the line from collar to shoulder point should look smooth, not lumpy.

WR|Shoulders|The seam hangs past your shoulder bone, and there is a dent in the top of the sleeve, as if the shoulder pad has nothing under it. Or the seam sits inside the shoulder bone and the sleeve pulls across the top of the arm.|The seam ends at the shoulder bone. The sleeve hangs straight and smooth from the top. You can lift your arms to shoulder height without the whole jacket rising up to your ears.

!WARN If the shoulders are wrong, do not buy the jacket, however good the price. Narrowing or widening shoulders is complicated, costly work and often does not look right afterwards. Every other jacket problem in this chapter is easier.

### Jacket check 2: Collar

- The jacket collar should lie flat against the back of your shirt collar.
- You should see about half an inch of shirt collar above the jacket collar at the back.
- A gap between jacket collar and shirt collar, sometimes called *collar gap*, is common in men whose posture has changed.
- A roll of cloth bunching just below the collar at the back means the jacket is pulling or the back is too long for your posture.

WR|Collar|A gap you could slide two fingers into at the back of the neck. Or a horizontal roll of cloth just under the collar.|The jacket collar hugs the shirt collar all the way around. A thin line of white shirt collar shows above it at the back.

!NOTE Collar gaps and rolls can often be improved by a skilled tailor, because they are caused by posture rather than a size mistake. Ask, rather than assume. It is a common job.

### Jacket check 3: Chest and button

Button the jacket (the top button of a two-button jacket, the middle button of a three-button). Now look.

- The front should close smoothly with no strain.
- An **X-shaped pull** radiating out from the button means it is too tight.
- If the jacket closes but the lapels lift away from your chest, it is also too tight across the chest.
- If you can pinch more than about two inches of cloth at either side of the button, it is too loose.
- Hug yourself. If the back seam strains hard, the jacket is too small for daily wear.

WR|Chest and button|An X of creases from the button. Or the front flaps open and the jacket looks like a box, with lots of loose cloth at the sides.|The front lies flat. The jacket narrows gently towards the waist and you can move your arms forward without strain.

### Jacket check 4: Sleeve length

- Arms hanging naturally at your sides, the sleeve should end at about the wrist bone.
- About a quarter to half an inch of shirt cuff should show.
- Bend your arm. The shirt cuff should still show, and the jacket sleeve should not ride up to the forearm.

WR|Sleeve length|The sleeve covers the shirt cuff completely and touches the base of the thumb or the knuckles.|The sleeve stops at the wrist bone. A thin line of white shirt cuff is visible: the detail that makes a jacket look as if it was made for you.

!TIP Your arms may be slightly different lengths. Many people's are. A tailor can shorten each sleeve to suit its own arm. Ask for both sleeves to be checked, not just one.

### Jacket check 5: Jacket length

- With arms down, the bottom of the jacket should roughly line up with the middle of your hand, or your knuckles if your fingers are curled.
- From the back, a traditional jacket covers, or nearly covers, the seat.
- Shorter, more fashionable jackets that stop well above the seat are rarely flattering after 60. They cut the body in an awkward place.

### Jacket check 6: The back

Turn sideways, then use the phone camera from behind.

- The back should hang smoothly from the collar to the hem.
- Vertical folds = too loose. Horizontal pull lines across the back = too tight.
- Vents (the slits at the back hem) should hang closed and straight when you stand. If they flare open, the jacket is too tight at the hips.

!WHY The back is the view everyone else sees and you never do. A smooth back says "careful man". A crumpled back says "this jacket gave up".

## Part B: Trousers

Trousers carry more fit problems than any other garment, mainly because the waist changes over time and most men keep buying the same size.

### Trouser check 1: Where the waistband sits

- Your natural waist is roughly level with the belly button, the soft narrowest part of your middle.
- Trousers should sit at or close to that point, held up by a properly fitted waistband.
- If the waistband slides under the stomach, the seat sags, the crotch drops and the legs look shorter.

WR|Waistband position|Belt buckle hidden under the stomach. Folds of cloth across the front of the thighs. The seat hangs low and loose.|Waistband at the natural waist. The trousers hang straight from the hip to the shoe, and the front crease falls in a clean line.

### Trouser check 2: Rise

The *rise* is the distance from the crotch seam to the top of the waistband. Most modern trousers are cut with a low rise, designed for younger bodies to be worn on the hips.

- A **mid or high rise** sits at the natural waist and is more comfortable for most men over 60.
- When trying on trousers, sit down. If the waistband digs in or slides down at the back, the rise is too low for you.

### Trouser check 3: Seat and thighs

- From behind, the cloth should follow the seat without pulling into horizontal lines (too tight) or hanging in a pouch (too loose).
- In the thighs, you should be able to pinch about an inch of cloth on either side.

### Trouser check 4: Length and break

The *break* is the soft fold that forms where the trouser hem meets the shoe.

| W=1.3,2.4,2.4 | Break | What it looks like | Verdict for men over 60 |
|---|---|---|---|
| No break | Hem just skims the top of the shoe, no fold | Clean and modern. Excellent if trousers are slim |
| Slight break | One small, soft fold at the front | **The safest, most flattering choice** |
| Full break | One deep fold | Acceptable for traditional wide trousers |
| Pooling | Several folds, cloth resting on the shoe | Always wrong. Looks borrowed and shortens the leg |

- At the back, the hem should reach roughly to the top of the shoe's heel, without dragging on the ground.
- Check length *wearing the shoes you will wear with the trousers*. Heel heights differ.

WR|Trouser length|Hem piles up in folds on top of the shoe and drags on the ground at the back. The ankle looks heavy and the legs look short.|The front of the hem just touches the shoe with at most one small fold. The legs look longer and the outfit looks deliberate.

### Trouser check 5: Leg width

- Straight-leg trousers suit nearly every man over 60.
- Very slim, tapered legs look strained on most mature bodies. Very wide legs look dated and add bulk.
- A useful guide: the hem opening should cover about two-thirds of the top of your shoe.

## Part C: Shirts

### Shirt check 1: Collar

1. Button the top button.
2. Slide one finger, flat, between the collar and the side of your neck.
3. One finger fits comfortably: correct. No finger: too tight. Two or three fingers: too loose.

| W=1.3,2.5,2.4 | Result | What you see | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Too tight | Collar digs in, skin folds over it, tie knot pulls crooked | Buy the next collar size up |
| Correct | Collar sits flat, tie knot sits centred | Note the size and keep buying it |
| Too loose | Collar gapes, stands away from the neck, tie slides | Buy a smaller collar size |

!WARN Collars cannot be enlarged or reduced well. This is one of the few problems a tailor cannot fix. Re-measure your neck every couple of years, because necks change.

### Shirt check 2: Shoulders and body

- Shoulder seam at the shoulder bone, same as a jacket.
- Tuck the shirt in and raise your arms. If the shirt pulls out of the trousers, the body is too short.
- Billowing at the waist when tucked in? The body is too full. Side seams can be taken in.
- Buttons pulling across the chest or stomach, with gaps between them? Too tight.

### Shirt check 3: Sleeves

- With arms down, the shirt cuff should reach the base of the thumb, where the hand meets the wrist.
- The cuff should be snug enough not to slide over the hand.
- With a jacket on, about a quarter to half an inch of cuff should show.

!TIP Shirt sleeves that are too long can be shortened, usually from the shoulder or by moving the cuff. It is worth doing on good shirts you wear often.

## Part D: Coats

A coat goes over everything else, so it must be checked *wearing a jacket or your thickest sweater*. A coat that fits over a shirt in the shop will be too tight in winter.

### Coat checks

- **Shoulders:** same rule as the jacket, but with a little more room. The coat shoulder should sit just beyond the jacket shoulder underneath.
- **Sleeves:** long enough to cover the jacket sleeve and shirt cuff completely, ending at the base of the thumb.
- **Length:** a coat that ends at or just below the knee is the most versatile and flattering for most men. Very short coats look sporty; very long ones can overwhelm a shorter man.
- **Closure:** button it over your jacket. The front should close without strain and without the X-pull.
- **Arm movement:** reach forward as if for a car door. The back should not feel as though it will split.

WR|Coat fit|A coat bought big "to be safe": shoulders drooping, sleeves to the fingertips, the body hanging like a tent.|Shoulders just past your own, sleeves covering the jacket cuff, a clean line from shoulder to hem. It should look like a coat, not a blanket.

## Part E: Sweaters and knitwear

- **Shoulders:** the seam at or just past your shoulder bone. Dropped shoulder seams far down the arm look sloppy.
- **Length:** the hem should reach the belt, covering the waistband, without falling to the hips.
- **Body:** the knit should skim the body. Clinging shows every contour; a balloon adds weight.
- **Cuffs and hem:** the ribbing should be snug and spring back. Stretched-out ribbing is the first sign a sweater has reached retirement (see Chapter 9).
- **Sleeves:** cuffs at the wrist bone, or able to be pushed up and stay there.

!NOTE Knitwear cannot be altered in the same way as tailored cloth. Fit it right in the shop, and look after it at home.

In the winter of 1986, in Namba, Osaka, my boss pointed at an old woman coming out of a bank with an envelope in her coat. He said one word. "Her." I sent two of my young men after her. On the fourth block, under a street light, she did one thing. And both of them came back to me with nothing.
#
I have thought about that woman for forty years. Because she was small. She was old. She walked with a cane. On paper, she was the easiest target in Osaka. And my men would not touch her.
#
Today, I will show you exactly what she knew. Seven things that made men like mine walk past a woman and choose someone else. None of them need a gun. None of them need strength. Most of them cost nothing at all. And stay with me to number one, because that is what the woman in Namba did. It has nothing to do with what was in her hand.
#
I'm Reiko Kuroda. For thirty-five years, I was Yakuza, the Japanese mafia. My job was not to fight. My job was to choose. I stood on corners and pointed at people, and my young men followed them. I was very good at it. It was wrong, and I will carry that. Now, I teach women like you what I used to look for. Not because you are weak. Because nobody ever told you how we chose.
#
Here is the secret first. We were not brave men. We were lazy men looking for an easy afternoon. My old driver, Shin, had a rule, and it became mine. Time. Noise. Eyes. Anything that made the job take longer, anything that made noise, anything that brought people looking. If a woman gave us even one of those three, we went somewhere else. Everything I show you today buys you one of them.
#
Number seven. Your keys, held the right way. Not like claws between your fingers. That is the movie version, and it fails. At my Saturday class, a new student named Doris, seventy-eight, a retired librarian, showed me her key claw with real pride. I had her punch a padded bag. The keys twisted, cut her own fingers, and the whole ring hit the floor. Now she had no weapon, and no way into her house.
#
So here is the right way. Have your keys out before you reach the door or the car. Not at the car. Before. My young men loved a woman standing at a car door, digging through her purse, with her back to the world. And on your car remote, there is a red button. The panic button. Press it, and your car screams for you. Time and noise, for free.
#
Number six. Your head up, and your eyes counting. Umeda, Osaka, 2004. Shin and I sat in a parking garage, watching a woman in her seventies walk to her car. Her keys were already in her hand. Her head was up. She looked at every parked car as she passed it. Shin put down his coffee and said, "She's counting us." We left.
#
My young men chose in the first three seconds. And they chose the woman looking down at a phone, into a bag, or under a pair of headphones. Learn the warning signs. A man who speeds up to close the gap. A man who matches your pace. A man standing where there is no reason to stand. And a friendly question, "Excuse me, do you have the time?" asked from a little too close. That question is not a question. It is a test, to see if you will let him in. If your stomach goes cold, believe it.
#
Number five. A small, bright light. The Rokko mountain road above Kobe, winter 2003. Shin stopped our car near a big house with no lights on. Then a porch light snapped on. An old woman stepped out in her slippers, pointed a flashlight straight at our license plate, and wrote something on a notepad. Shin put the car in gear, and never drove that road again. He hated two things. A light in his face, and a pencil.
#
A kitchen drawer flashlight is too weak. A good one throws several hundred lumens, and it costs about twenty dollars. At night, put that beam straight into his eyes. He blinks, he turns his head, and for two seconds he cannot see you well. Two seconds is a head start. Keep one in your purse, and one on your nightstand.
#
Number four. Noise that no one can take from you. My granddaughter clipped a little personal alarm to my purse in a Seattle parking lot, two winters ago. I went digging for my shopping list and pulled the pin by mistake. It shrieked. Every head in that parking lot turned. A man two rows over put down his bags and started walking toward me to help. I was so embarrassed. And I stood there thinking, this is what my young men were afraid of for thirty years.
#
Most of these scream at about a hundred and twenty decibels. Nothing to aim. No training. No strength. Pull the pin, let it scream, and walk toward people. Test it once a month. Ten to twenty dollars. A loud old woman cost us more than any locked door ever did.
#
Number three. The space between you. When I was twelve, my Uncle Mori drew a chalk line across his dojo floor in Osaka, and stood me on one side of it. He said, "The fight is decided before anyone touches. Whoever controls the distance decides it." The old sword schools had a word for that distance. Ma-ai.
#
A man about one parking space away can reach you faster than you can open a purse. So when someone moves toward you, step back, and to the side. Put something between you. A car. A bench. A shopping cart. My young men needed to be close. Every step you take back is a step he has to take in front of witnesses.
#
Number two. Your voice. Tsuruhashi Market, Osaka, 1978. A pickle seller named Missus Kanda, seventy years old, with a voice like a ferry horn. One of our young men leaned over her cash box. She put both palms up in his face and roared, "Back up!" Fifty faces in that market turned around at once. He backed up. Nobody touched her stall again.
#
Put your hands up, open and flat, like you are telling a car to stop backing into you. Open hands protect your face, and they show every witness that you want peace, not a fight. Then one short command, loud enough that someone half a block away looks over. "Back up!" A man who means to hurt you needs two things. Quiet, and you alone. Your voice takes both.
#
Now, let me ask you something. If you could do only one thing from this list tomorrow, which one would you bet on? Your keys, your eyes, the light, the alarm, your space, or your voice? Tell me in the comments, and tell me why. I will give you my answer at the end.
#
Number one. A decision you make ahead of time. Here is what I promised you. On that fourth block in Namba, the old woman stopped under the street light. She did not run. She did not reach for her envelope. She turned around, square to my two young men, set her feet, and lifted her cane in front of her. Then she said, very quietly, "Not tonight, boys." Her hands were steady. They were forty years younger and twice her size. They walked away.
#
Years later, I learned she had been robbed once before, and she had promised her husband it would never go the same way twice. She decided long before we ever saw her. The tool never saves you. The decision does.
#
The real danger for most women is not the man. It is freezing. So decide now, while you are calm in your chair. Picture him stepping toward you. Picture your hands coming up, your feet stepping back, and your voice saying, "Back up." If he grabs you anyway, you are not trying to beat him. You are trying to make his hands open. One hard strike with whatever is in your hand, and then you go toward people and light. Uncle Mori used to say, "The dojo teaches control. This is control." You decided before he did.
#
Now, the law. I am a retired old woman, not your lawyer. Every state writes its own rules, so read yours before you carry anything. You may defend yourself with force that fits the danger you are really in, and only while it is happening. The moment he stops, or runs, you stop. You do this to get free, never to get even. Then you get to people, and you are the first one to call the police.
#
So here is everything in one breath. Time, noise, and eyes. Keys out before the car. Head up, eyes counting. A bright light at night. An alarm on your keys. Keep your space. Use your voice. And make your decision today, so you do not have to make it on the worst night of your life.
#
Earlier, I asked which one you would choose. My answer is number one. The decision. Because a woman who has already decided stands differently, looks differently, and sounds differently. And men like mine could see it from across the street. That is why we never touched her.
#
Here is your job this week, and it takes ten minutes. Tonight, put a flashlight on your nightstand. Pick one thing you will carry, give it one pocket, the same pocket every day, and reach for it ten times with your eyes closed. Then stand in your kitchen, put your hands up, and say "Back up" out loud. Once. Louder than feels polite.
#
Now tell me something. What is in your purse right now that you would reach for? And what part of the country are you watching from? I read every one. If someone you love walks to her car alone at night, subscribe, and send her this video. There is another video on your screen right now. Watch that one next. Lock your door. Live long. I will see you next time.

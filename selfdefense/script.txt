If you ever have to hit a man to save your life, and you go for his face with your fist, the first bone that breaks may be your own.
#
In my old life, there was a bone setter in Nada named Doctor Ishikawa. He worked out of a back room behind a pharmacy, and he never asked questions. One winter, in 1996, he set the hands of eleven of my young men. Eleven. Strong boys, every one of them. He told me, "Kuroda-san, your boys keep fighting with the smallest bones they own."
#
Now, last week I told you to put the heel of your palm under a big man's nose. I am not taking back one word. But a palm is not a fist. And today I will show you exactly when going for the head makes sense, and when it is the most foolish thing you can do. You will learn three spots on a big man's body that can drop him, with no gloves and no great strength. And stay with me to the end, because there is one thing on his body that tells you which spot to choose. It is not his face. Almost nobody looks at it.
#
I'm Reiko Kuroda. For thirty-five years, I was Yakuza, the Japanese mafia. I was the woman people like you were afraid of. It paid well, and it was wrong. But I knew exactly which people we picked, and which ones we left alone. Now, I teach it to the people we used to target. Not because you were weak. Because nobody ever showed you.
#
Picture it. No gloves. No referee. You swing at his face, and he moves his head an inch. Your knuckles land on his forehead. The front of a man's skull is a helmet. It was built to take a blow. Your hand has twenty-seven small bones, and they were built to button a shirt. Boxers punch heads all night, but they wrap their hands in tape and pad them in gloves. You will have neither. And past sixty, our bones are thinner than they were. A broken hand in the first second means you have no hands at all for the rest of it.
#
And here is the worst outcome. Say your punch lands perfectly. He falls backward, and his head hits the concrete. Now it is not self-defense anymore. A young man in my group threw one punch outside Sannomiya Station in 1999. One punch. One curb. He did six years. So the first rule is the oldest one. If you can walk away, you walk away. Only if he takes that choice from you, do you need what comes next.
#
My young men counted on one thing in a fight. They counted on the other person swinging at their face. Because a face is easy to protect. Hands up, chin down. Done. What they could not protect was lower.
#
Number three. The liver. Put your right hand on your right side, just below your bottom ribs. Press in a little. Feel that? Something uncomfortable, and no muscle over it. That is your liver, and every man has one in the same place, no matter how big he is. A big man's chest and stomach are like hitting a bear. Muscle, ribs, maybe a good layer of fat. But every suit of armor has a gap, and this is the gap on his body. Boxers with stomachs like steel go down from one well-placed blow there. They cannot help it. The body simply sits down.
#
Here is how, after sixty. You do not need a fist. Use the heel of your palm, or up close, your elbow. Turn your hips into it, the way you turn to close a heavy car door with your hip. The power comes from that turn, not from your arm. And two mistakes to avoid. Do not wind up. A big swing, or a dip of your shoulder before you strike, tells him it is coming, and he will cover it. And do not hit the skin. Aim a few inches inside him, like you are reaching for something behind his ribs. A blow that stops at the skin is a slap. A blow that goes through is the one that drops him.
#
Uncle Mori taught me this when I was fourteen. There was a student named Kuribayashi, a carpenter's son, so wide he filled the doorway. Nobody could move him. One evening, in the middle of sparring, my uncle stepped in and touched Kuribayashi's right side, no harder than tapping a melon. The big boy sat down on the mat with a strange look on his face. Uncle Mori turned to me and said, "Muscle is a coat, Reiko. Every coat has a gap under the arm." The lesson. You do not need to be stronger than his muscle. You only need to find the gap in his coat.
#
But what if he is not standing still? What if he is running straight at you? Then there is a spot that lets his own speed do your work.
#
Number two. The solar plexus. Find where your ribs meet in the middle of your chest. Now go about two fingers below it. Press there, and notice your breath catch. That soft spot is a bundle of nerves just under the surface. Have you ever been hit there, or fallen hard on your stomach, and for a few seconds you could not breathe at all? That is what he will feel.
#
The wrong instinct when a big man charges is to back up. He is faster forward than you are backward. So instead, as he comes, bring your palm up into that spot, and hold your arm firm, like stopping a door that is swinging shut on you. Push slightly through him. You do not have to swing at all. His own weight and his own speed run straight into your hand. The one honest thing about this spot: it is small. An inch too high, and you hit breastbone. An inch to the side, and you hit ribs. Near the spot does very little. It has to be the spot.
#
In my Saturday class, there is a woman named Bernice, seventy-four, who was a nurse for forty years. When I showed the class this spot, she laughed. "Reiko, I know that spot. In CPR, we teach people never to press there." Then she found it on herself in one try. Every other student took three or four. She looked at me and said, "So I've been carrying a weapon around for forty years, and nobody told me." The lesson. The target was always there. Knowing exactly where it is turns a small hand into a big answer.
#
My men, when they rushed someone, counted on that person stepping back. A person who held their ground and put a hand out, they ran into it with all their own weight. Now, a question for you. If a big man is running at you, which would you choose? The liver, or the solar plexus? Write it in the comments, and tell me why. I will give you my answer in a minute, because the third spot, the one most people would never think of, may beat them both.
#
Number one. The leg. Here is a secret about fights. Almost every blow goes to the head. So his hands are up, and his eyes are up, watching your hands. Nobody is watching the legs. The wrong instinct comes from the movies. A high kick to the head. Please, do not. In real life, in slacks and shoes, with no warm-up, on concrete, at our age, the person who throws a movie kick is usually the person who ends up on the ground.
#
So, you go low. Keep your foot no higher than a curb. Swing your shin, or drive the bottom of your shoe into the outside of his thigh, halfway between his hip and his knee, like kicking a stuck cabinet door shut. Short, hard, and your foot is back on the ground before he knows what happened. You have felt this as a child. Somebody gave you a dead leg, and you could not run for a minute. That is the idea. A man with a dead leg cannot chase you. He cannot plant his feet to hit you. His balance goes, and his size goes with it. And you never stood on one leg for longer than a blink.
#
I learned this one watching it happen to my own man. Shinsekai, Osaka, summer of 1981. One of our young men went to lean on an old fish seller, sixty-six, in white rubber boots. The young man was watching the old man's hands, because he thought the fish knife was the danger. The old man never touched the knife. He kicked my man once, low, in the side of the thigh, and went back to his fish. My man limped home and would not say why. We never went to that stall again. The lesson. The big man is watching your hands. Use your feet.
#
Now, I promised you the one thing that tells you which spot to choose. It is his hands. My husband, Michio, taught me this. In my old life, we sometimes sat at dinners with very dangerous men. Michio never watched their faces. Afterward, he would tell me everything. "Reiko, faces lie. Hands don't. The man at the end of the table kept one hand under it all night." He was right, every time.
#
So, watch his hands. If his hands are high, guarding his face, then his face is the wall. Do not hit the wall. Go to his body, his solar plexus and his liver, and go to his leg. If his hands drop low, his face is open. Then you may go high, but never the forehead. Go for the jaw, and never with a fist. Use the heel of your palm, fingers bent back, so the solid base of your hand does the work. It does not break. And if he is so close you cannot reach with your arm, use your elbow. Short and straight.
#
Here is the scary truth. When it happens, you will not think. Your body does what it has already done a thousand times, and nothing else. Miyamoto Musashi, the greatest swordsman in Japanese history, wrote that a thousand days of training forge you, and ten thousand days polish you. You do not need ten thousand days. You need a few minutes every day.
#
And the law. I am a black belt and a retired old crook, not your lawyer. Every state writes its own rules. You may defend yourself with force that fits the danger you are really in. The moment he stops, or turns, or runs, you stop. You do this to get free, never to get even. Then you get to people and light, and you call for help from somewhere safe. In my old life, the men who went to prison were not the ones who defended themselves. They were the ones who kept going after it was over. Remember that young man at Sannomiya Station.
#
So here is the thing I promised. His hands decide. Hands high: solar plexus, liver, leg. Hands low: the heel of your palm to his jaw. Too close: your elbow. And never a bare fist to his head. My answer to the question? Neither. I choose the leg. It is the safest for you. It is the one he is not watching. And it works no matter how big he is.
#
Here is your one thing this week. Fold a couch cushion and tape it to a doorframe at about stomach height. Every day, ten slow palm strikes into it, turning your hips. Then ten low kicks to the bottom of the frame. Foot no higher than a curb. Five minutes a day. Then tell me in the comments what changed after one week. And tell me, has anyone ever tried to push you around because they were bigger? How did you handle it? And what part of the country are you in? I read every one.
